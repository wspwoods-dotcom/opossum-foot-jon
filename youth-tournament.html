The Youth Trapping Tournament Plan

One week, in season, on real traplines. Kids log every check in the app, species score on a weighted points table, and one card draw can turn an opossum into gold. Built on New Zealand’s Trap.NZ and Karekare contest model, Jon Cook’s card-draw idea, and Iowa law. Everything marked as a plan choice is ours to set; everything marked NZ research is sourced.

The format, on one table

The NZ contests kept it simple enough to explain on one rules page — Karekare ran three months on raw counts with four trap types allowed and a photo for every catch. We keep that simplicity and add the two things the NZ model never had: a weighted points table and the card draw.

The week, day by day

Seven days means seven consecutive mornings of checks — a real commitment for families, and the honest core of the event. The week is built around that rhythm: teach first, set second, check daily, finish together in person.

  1. Before the week Registration & trapper ed

    Parent creates the account and adds the youth as a sub-profile (see the legal gates below). Youth completes Iowa trapper education or traps under a licensed mentor where the law allows. Each entrant registers their sets in the app and gets a personal tournament card — name and entrant number — that must appear in every catch photo. That card-in-photo system is borrowed from the Angling Ireland Species Hunt, the clearest published photo-verification workflow the research found. Angling Ireland species hunt guide.

  2. Saturday, Nov 14 Kickoff & the draw

    Morning kickoff: safety and ethics briefing, reading sign, where sets belong (Karekare coached hotspots, bait, and trap placement on its rules page — we do the same, in person). Then the moment the whole week turns on: the Golden Species card is drawn, in front of everybody, and sealed into the scoring. Sets go in that afternoon.

  3. Sun Nov 15 – Fri Nov 20 The line runs

    Daily checks inside every 24 hours, every check logged — catch or empty. Judges review photos through the week, so the leaderboard is real, not a pile of claims waiting for the finish. A midweek check-in message names the current leaders in each division and reminds everyone the lucky draw rewards complete logging: a week of honest empty checks still earns entries.

  4. Saturday, Nov 21 Final check & weigh-in

    Last morning check, then all sets pulled. In-person final check in the afternoon: entrants bring pelts (or the photo set plus pelt) for verification, biggest-catch measuring with a ruler in the photo, and fur-handling judging. This is the NZ hunting-comp norm — North Canterbury and the Kids Hunt both verify in person, with rules against frozen, farmed, or poisoned animals and the head judge’s call final. App-only verification is where Karekare’s public record goes quiet, and it’s the gap we close. North Canterbury rules; North Canterbury Kids Hunt.

  5. Saturday evening Prize-giving

    Division winners, Overall champion, species titles, Best Fur Handling, the lucky draw, and the Golden Species reveal of what the card was worth. Every entrant who logged all seven days of checks gets the completion award, win or lose — the Karekare poster-at-three-catches principle, pointed at effort instead of luck.

The points table

Here’s the honest state of the research: no NZ trapping contest uses a points table at all — they count raw catches. The one clean NZ multi-species table that exists is the NZ Bowhunters Society’s lifetime-award table. Our table adapts its one published ratio — a hard-to-trap mustelid is worth eight rats — to Iowa furbearers, following the research’s suggested shape: beginner species at 1–2, mink and beaver mid-table, fox and coyote high, bobcat and otter at the top.

The Golden Species: Jon Cook’s card draw

Jon runs his Nebraska thermal predator hunts on a points system with a twist: draw cards, and the luck of the draw can make a lowly species worth the most — an opossum could outscore everything and level the field between a kid on a creek bank and a veteran on prime ground. The research chased this format hard, and the answer matters for how we run it.

No real contest anywhere was found that does this. Across trapping, hunting, and fishing rulebooks, no tournament revalues a species by card draw. That makes Jon’s idea genuinely new — there is no proven rulebook to copy, and no fairness study behind it. What does exist are three near-cousins, and each one deliberately keeps the randomness small or separate so a lucky draw can’t overturn a week of work.

Mystery species, announced late

Minnesota’s Brainerd Jaycees Ice Fishing Extravaganza names a “mystery fish” no more than 24 hours before the contest — walleye in 2026, sunfish in 2024, perch in 2023 — as a side contest with its own prize, verified by officials, worth a package valued near $9,000. It never touches the main standings.

icefishing.org — Catch of the Day

Mystery fish, kept out of the total

Florida’s Renegades of Reel Estate Classic drew its mystery fish (cobia) at the captains’ meeting and paid $250 for the largest — with the rule stated outright: the mystery fish does not count toward the overall aggregate.

renegadesofreelestate.com/rules (index-level source)

Wild cards at a flat 1 point

The Gulf Coast Fly Fishing Mixed Bag Classic scores 20 species at 1–4 points and lets any unlisted species enter as a wild card — worth a flat 1 point. Breadth gets rewarded; an oddball catch can’t run away with the week.

2025 GCC Mixed Bag Classic overview (PDF)

Recommended: the sealed bonus Plan choice

Draw the card at kickoff, in front of everyone, from a deck of the scored species. The golden species earns +5 per catch, capped at +15 total, on top of its base points. Announced at the start, it’s strategy — a kid can choose to work opossum sign all week — not a lottery revealed after the fact. Drawn after the catches are logged, the research warns, it stops being a contest twist and becomes a raffle stapled to a merit contest, and the rules would have to say so. The sandbox’s middle setting runs exactly this rule.

Jon’s full-strength version Open decision

The full draw — golden species jumps to the top value (10 points a catch in the sandbox) — is the most fun version and the riskiest. Run the sandbox with opossum drawn and watch Sam’s two-species creek week bury June’s coyote. That flip is the point of the idea and its danger: it rewards whoever happens to sit on the golden species’ habitat. If we run it full-strength, the honest place for it is a headline side title — Golden Species Champion — with its own prize, while the Overall still crowns the best all-around week. That split is exactly how Brainerd and Renegades protect their main event.

How a catch counts

Karekare ran on trust: log it on Trap.NZ, load a photo. For a US kids’ tournament with real prizes, the plan layers the NZ logging model with fishing-tournament photo rules and the NZ hunting comps’ in-person finish. Every layer below traces to a source in the research.

The logging rules

  • Scan at the set.Each registered set carries a code; scanning it opens that set’s check form, pre-filled with date and time — the Trap.NZ QR model, where every trap is auto-assigned a unique code.
  • Log every check, empty or not.Trap.NZ’s own guidance is to log the empty traps too. A week of catch-only logs is a red flag; a full check history is what earns lucky-draw entries.
  • Photo in the app, card in the frame.Catch photo taken in-app (not uploaded from the gallery) showing the animal and the entrant’s personal tournament card in one frame. Size titles need a ruler or tape in the shot — Karekare’s monster-rat rule.
  • Works with no signal.Rural lines have dead zones; Trap.NZ’s offline sync is the feature the whole model depends on. Logs queue on the phone and sync at home.
  • Judges approve before points post.A human review queue, like Angling Ireland’s host approval, moves the leaderboard. Duplicate animals, photos of photos, altered timestamps, and logs outside the boundary or season window are rejected.

The finish & the fraud list

  • In-person final check.Pelts (or pelt plus photo set) presented Saturday afternoon. Biggest-catch titles measured on the spot.
  • Disqualifiers, published in advance.Frozen, farmed, pen-reared, rotten, or poisoned animals; animals taken outside the tournament window or boundary; the same animal entered twice or by two entrants. Borrowed straight from the North Canterbury and Kids Hunt rulebooks.
  • Head judge’s call is final.Named coordinator, named judges, spot-checks on the line during the week.
  • Tags and reports still apply.Tournament rules sit on top of Iowa law, never instead of it — species that require tags or harvest reports get them, on the state’s clock.
  • Kids’ locations never go public.Leaderboards show first names and totals only. Exact set locations stay inside the project; any public map aggregates, NZ hexmap-style.

Prizes: experiences and gear, not cash

The NZ pattern is consistent: the top prizes are things a family does together, the threshold to win something is low, and a draw keeps the unlucky in the game. Cash for kids is the one proven flashpoint — North Canterbury’s 2023 NZ$250 prize for the child who killed the most feral cats was dropped after animal-welfare backlash, and the lesson from the research is that species choice and pet-safety rules, not scoring, carry the reputational risk.

The gates that decide if this runs

NZ’s model works because its targets are introduced pests that can be trapped year-round with no licence. None of that is true here, and the plan doesn’t pretend otherwise. Four gates, all checked before a date is announced.

1 · Iowa law, not a national rule Must verify

US furbearers are native, state-managed game. Licences, trapper education, seasons, trap types, setbacks, and tag/report duties are set state by state, and the research’s state examples (Kentucky, South Carolina, Arizona, New York, Virginia) differ on nearly every point. The tournament state wasn’t fixed when the research ran — this plan assumes Iowa, and every rule in it must be confirmed against the current Iowa DNR regulation booklet before sign-ups open: youth licence and mentor requirements, which species need tags, and check intervals for each trap type.

2 · Seven days means seven checks

Most states, Iowa included in the general pattern, require every trap visited at least once in each 24-hour period. A weeklong tournament is seven consecutive days of checking, in November weather, before school. Families sign up for that with eyes open — it’s stated on the registration page, not discovered on day four. If a family can’t check, a registered mentor or partner checks the line, the Karekare rule for holidays.

3 · Kids’ data: parent holds the account COPPA

An app directed at under-13s triggers COPPA: verifiable parental consent before collecting a child’s personal information, which includes photos and geolocation. The amended FTC rule (published Apr 22, 2025; compliance Apr 22, 2026) adds separate consent for third-party disclosure. So: the parent is the account holder, the youth is a sub-profile, no photos of children (catches only), no public full names, no public locations, age gate at sign-up. Trap.NZ has no documented parent/child flow — this layer is ours to build, not copy.

JD Supra — amended COPPA rule (index-level source)

4 · Land, supervision, species care

Written landowner permission for every line not on family land. Adult supervision at all times for junior entrants — the standing rule at North Canterbury and the Kids Hunt, whose organisers are explicit that families arrange their own trips, permissions, and supervision. And the North Canterbury lesson: publish pet-safety and setback rules up front (distance from homes and trails, neighbour notification where sets are near houses), because that’s where public concern actually lands.

Pair it with education, don’t replace it. The proven US kids’ model is Gardner Camp in Illinois: a two-day camp since 2017, 20–25 students, the full state trapper-education course built in, species-group field sessions, and fur-handling instruction — created because the average Illinois trapper’s age rose from 46 (2014–15) to 54 (2023–24). A tournament week bolted onto a trapper-ed weekend recruits the same families and sends every kid home licensed and taught, not just ranked.

What the app has to do

Trap.NZ is the logging template, with one documented gap: it has no contest, leaderboard, or points module — Karekare’s organisers exported the totals and awarded prizes by hand. Tournament scoring has to be built on top of the logging layer, in Opossum Foot or beside it. The build list, in running order:

Logging layer (Trap.NZ-shaped)

  • A tournament project containing every entrant’s registered sets
  • A scannable code per set that opens its check form directly
  • Check form: species, set status, rebaited + bait used, photo, auto date/time — and an explicit “checked, nothing caught” entry
  • Offline-first logging with sync when signal returns
  • Per-entrant “my week” view: catches, checks, species count

Tournament layer (the new build)

  • Parent account with youth sub-profiles and consent flow
  • Judge review queue: approve / reject with a note, before points post
  • Scoring engine: this points table, per-species cap, breadth bonus, golden-species rule — all set by the organiser, not hard-coded
  • Leaderboard by division + overall, first names only
  • Lucky-draw ledger: one entry per complete logged day
  • CSV export of every log for the final audit and the record book

Scale note, quoted with its dates because the sources disagree: Predator Free NZ (updated Sep 14, 2026) puts Trap.NZ at 35,000 people, nearly 13 million records, and 5,700+ projects; a DOC/LiveNews item dated Nov 6, 2026 says 70,000+ members, 26 million records, and 10,000+ projects. The counts are defined differently (people vs. registered members), so neither number stands alone — what’s solid is that the model runs at national scale and costs its users nothing: Trap.NZ is a free service, apps included. predatorfreenz.org — why logging matters; Trap.NZ user guide.

Open decisions, in the order they block things

The plan is buildable as written, but five calls are genuinely open. Each is marked with what the research can and can’t settle.

  1. Confirm Iowa, and the Iowa rulebook answers.

    The research mission named no state, so no licence, tag, or youth-mentor answer in this plan is final. Confirm the host state, then verify youth licensing, mentor rules, tagged species, and check intervals against the current booklet.

  2. Golden Species: sealed bonus, or Jon’s full draw?

    No precedent exists either way — the format is untested in any real contest found. The plan defaults to the capped bonus for the Overall, with the full draw available as the separate Golden Species Champion title. This is the signature call of the whole event.

  3. Lock the points table numbers.

    The shape is research-grounded (Bowhunters’ 8:1 ratio, caps, breadth bonus); the exact Iowa values are a proposal. Settle them with the sandbox and local trappers who know Greene County lines — then publish them with the rules and don’t move them mid-week.

  4. Do pelts get sold?

    NZ contests have no pelt sale; a US fur tournament does. Decide in the rules whether entrants keep, sell, or turn in pelts — and whether a sold pelt still counts — because fur value (a mink at $25 one era, $9 in another, per the research’s general guide) quietly changes what kids target.

  5. Entry fee or free?

    NZ hunting comps charge (North Canterbury: $50 adult / $10 junior; the Te Ranga possum hunt: $20 a person), and every paid entry feeds their spot-prize draws. Karekare’s rules page names no fee. A small youth fee funds the lucky draw and completion awards; free entry maximises the school pipeline. Either works — it changes the prize budget, so it gets decided before sponsors are approached.
